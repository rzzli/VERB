This talk—"A New Age of Software Engineering" by Walden Yan, Co-founder and
Chief Product Officer at Cognition (the creators of Devin)—is the natural
companion to Lauren Tan’s talk. While Lauren focused heavily on codebase hygiene
and verification, Walden zooms out to organizational design, sandbox
infrastructure, recursive factory optimization, and delegating entire jobs (not
just tasks) to agents.

Here is a complete, deeply elaborated guide to every part of the talk, blending
what Cognition built with the underlying engineering concepts.

1. The Headline Metric: 40.4x Delivery Capacity

Over a 12-month period (Nov 2025 – Oct 2026):

  - Cognition’s merged PR throughput exploded 40.4x.
  - Over that same year, their engineering headcount grew only 2.7x.
  - Net impact: Every engineer at Cognition became ~15x more productive (40.4
    / 2.7), while maintaining PR quality that Walden argues is equal to or
    higher than before.

His core argument: You don’t get a 40x speedup just by buying a coding agent.
You get it by rebuilding both your software infrastructure and your engineering
org chart around agents.

2. The Software Factory & Restructuring Teams into "Vertical Pods"

"Default Autonomous; Human Judgment Where Needed"

Cognition treats their entire Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) as a
self-driving Software Factory across six stages: Triage → Build → Test → Review
→ Deploy → Oncall

  - By default, Devin runs every stage end-to-end: Triaging customer bug reports
    and deduplicating them, writing code, running unit/integration/live QA
    tests, performing automated Devin Review, running release pipelines, and
    even acting as first-responder during oncall (correlating Sentry/Datadog
    logs with recent commits and opening hotfix PRs).
  - Humans enter only via "Escalations": When product judgment, ambiguity
    resolution, or high-risk sign-off is required.

Why They Blew Up the Traditional Org Chart

In March 2026, Cognition’s leadership gave their engineers a blunt warning:

"If your workflow is picking tickets off a Linear board and sending them to an
agent to implement, you will not be set up for success in the age of AI."

Why? Because if a human can cleanly specify a ticket, an agent can already
receive that ticket directly—making the human middleman redundant.

To adapt, Cognition reorganized from horizontal functional silos into autonomous
vertical pods:

  - Before (By Role): Separate layers for Product (PM) → Engineering (4
    engineers) → QA & Oncall.
  - After (By Vertical): Small 2–3 person pods owning an entire product area
    (e.g., Billing, Mobile App, Integrations). Every person in the pod is a
    hybrid "Product Eng / Oncall" commanding a fleet of Devin agents.
  - The New Engineer's Job: Each engineer listens directly to customers, handles
    product management/prioritization, designs verification criteria, manages
    their own Linear board, and dispatches swarms of agents to execute the
    implementation. (In classic org-design terms, this turns every individual
    engineer into a miniature, full-stack "two-pizza team.")

3. Abstracting Away the IDE: Debugging, Testing & Merging from Slack

When your agents are reliable enough, the interface for software engineering
changes. Walden walks through a real production bug flow at Cognition:

1.  Customer reports a UI bug in a shared Slack channel (e.g., "file comment
    should scroll into view" with a screen recording showing "No comment
    appears").
2.  An engineer forwards the thread into #internal-bug-reports and tags @Devin.
3.  Devin replies in-thread with:
      - A ready PR with all CI checks passing and Devin Review complete.
      - An embedded verification video recorded on a live preview deployment
        proving the fix works ("Comment appears").
4.  The engineer reviews the 5-second video—never opening an IDE, an agent
    dashboard, or even GitHub—merges, and forwards the video proof straight back
    to the customer.

Key takeaway: Code review shifts from reading syntax diffs to reviewing
visual/behavioral evidence. Several Cognition engineers now do much of their
daily engineering work from iPads.

4. The Hard Infrastructure Part: Building an Agent-Testable Codebase

Walden emphasizes that Slack-to-PR magic is impossible unless you solve the
environment problem underneath. Agents fail most often not because they can't
write code, but because they can't run and verify the whole system safely.

1. Localizing Everything Into a Single VM

  - Before: Testing required connecting to AWS, staging databases, live
    GitHub/Slack/Teams webhooks, and live servers—meaning agents collided with
    each other, corrupted shared staging state, or lacked permissions.
  - After: Cognition ran a massive migration so every internal service runs
    locally inside docker compose on a single isolated virtual machine (VM), and
    every external 3rd-party dependency (AWS, Slack, Teams, GitHub, etc.) has a
    local mock. Every Devin gets its own self-contained replica of the entire
    company stack.

2. Hourly VM Snapshots (with Self-Healing Builds)

  - Spinning up and compiling a huge monorepo from scratch wastes 10+ minutes
    per agent session.
  - Cognition rebuilds the entire codebase from main into a fresh VM snapshot
    every hour. Every Slack-triggered Devin boots instantly into a warm,
    pre-built environment.
  - Self-healing snapshots: In a codebase merging hundreds of PRs a day, builds
    occasionally break. When an hourly snapshot build fails, Devin is
    automatically paged first to fix the broken build so human engineers don't
    have to babysit CI.

3. Self-Maintaining SKILL.md Files & Advanced "Multiplayer" Testing

  - Instead of humans manually updating QA scripts every time the UI changes,
    Devin maintains its own SKILL.md testing instructions, updating how to test
    features as the codebase evolves.
  - Complex Multi-Agent Testing (The Zoom Voice Call Example): When building
    Devin’s ability to join live Zoom calls, they didn't force humans to jump on
    test calls. Inside a single VM, they routed virtual audio devices
    (BlackHole 2ch), launched multiple test accounts in a meeting
    simultaneously, and had them speak voice prompts to each other ("This is
    Devin speaking on the Mac Devin, what is the capital of Spain?") to verify
    end-to-end audio/transcription/response loops autonomously.

4. The Underrated Bonus: Cloud-VM Security

Moving engineers off laptops stuffed with local .env files, SSH keys, and DevOps
tokens into ephemeral, sandboxed cloud VMs drastically shrinks the attack
surface for supply-chain and credential-leakage attacks.

5. Recursive Self-Improvement (At the System Level, Not the Model Level)

Most people hear "recursive self-improvement" and think of training new model
weights via reinforcement learning. Walden points out a much more practical form
available today: Factory-Level Recursive Self-Improvement.

Because every cloud agent session—every shell command, CI retry, tool call, and
failure—is logged, Cognition runs analyzer agents over thousands of past Devin
traces (1,300+ sessions analyzed in the demo) to hunt for systemic waste. Every
day, Devin posts concrete optimization proposals in Slack:

  - Eliminating Toolchain Drift: "Setup fails on Node drift → blueprint pins the
    toolchain" (Cut setup time from 9 min → 40 sec).
  - Cutting Redundant Compute: "Sessions re-run full CI on every small edit →
    tests scoped to the diff" (-18% cost).
  - Squashing Flaky Tests: "One flaky E2E suite retried 12+ times → root cause
    fixed" (0 retries).
  - Turning Repeated GUI Actions into Skills: "Login via computer-use repeated
    in 80% of test runs → saved as a reusable skill" (-31% test time).

In other words, your agents continuously optimize the environment for future
agents, compounding speed and lowering token costs automatically.

6. The Next Paradigm: "We Give Agents Jobs, Not Tasks"

This is Walden’s biggest conceptual leap. Most teams use AI like a contractor
receiving micro-tickets ("Add an index to the orders table"). Cognition argues
you should treat an agent like an autonomous employee owning a KPI.

From Delegating Tasks → Delegating Outcomes

1.  Human expresses a desire: "Make our P99 endpoint latency faster."
2.  Agent defines goals & KPIs: Measures baseline (840ms), proposes a target
    (500ms), and requests a compute budget (5,000 ACUs approved).
3.  Agent scans and creates its own backlog: Audits the codebase and generates
    14 issues (batching order lookups, adding missing DB indexes, setting up
    Datadog alerts if P99 > 1s, scheduling a weekly Slack report).
4.  Agent executes, monitors, and reports: Ships fixes, watches production
    telemetry for regressions, and sends daily metric summaries to the
    engineer's phone ("P99 is down 45%. No regressions.").

Walden’s Rule of Thumb: Anything you think isn't measurable, find a way to
measure it—click latency, UI layout shifts (by measuring DOM bounding-box
movement), onboarding retention, CI duration—and put an agent on a 24/7 loop to
optimize that number.

7. Two Real-World Case Studies of "Job Delegation"

Case Study 1: 2.8x Inference Speedup on NVIDIA Vera Rubin GPUs

Cognition was one of the first recipients of NVIDIA's new Vera Rubin GPUs—so
early that optimized inference kernels didn't even exist yet. Instead of
dedicating a large systems team to hand-tune CUDA kernels for months, they
allocated a permanent cluster of GPUs to Devin with a single goal: maximize
output tokens/minute per GPU (TPM) on their agentic workload.

Over ~7 days (Sep 20 – Sep 26), Devin hypothesized optimizations, benchmarked
them on the real hardware, and sent winning patches to human engineers for
approval. Each step stacked on the last (with standard LLM inference engineering
context):

  - Starting point: ~9k TPM (slower than the previous generation GB200 / GB300
    baselines).

1.  Merged GPU-to-GPU steps (+15%): Fusing collective communication/kernel
    launches to reduce inter-GPU synchronization overhead.
2.  Batched draft-model guesses (+15%): Optimizing speculative decoding (using a
    small fast draft model to propose multiple tokens verified in parallel by
    the main model).
3.  Grouped incoming prompts (+9%): Improved request/prefix batching to boost
    arithmetic intensity.
4.  Pre-recorded GPU tasks (+7%): Using CUDA Graphs to capture and replay static
    kernel sequences with near-zero CPU launch overhead.
5.  More conversations at once (72, +9%) & More memory, less waiting (96, +20%):
    Increasing continuous batching concurrency and KV-cache memory utilization
    so GPU compute cores don't sit idle waiting on memory bandwidth.
6.  Faster memory clock (+8%) & Automated tuning (120, +16%): Hardware clock
    tuning and automated kernel/tile-size parameter sweeps.

Final Result: 23,600+ TPM (a 2.8x gain), vastly outperforming the previous-gen
GB300 peak (17,185) in just one week.

Case Study 2: Building "Devin for Android" From Scratch in 44 Days

Cognition had an iOS team actively shipping features, but lacked the headcount
to staff an Android team. So an engineer named Samyok gave Devin one overarching
job:

"Your job is to product manage the Android app... basically, you're going to
autonomously create the entire thing, but we need to figure out what the
blockers are for you doing that first."

  - How the loop ran: They wired permanent hooks into the iOS repo so every new
    iOS feature triggered Devin to build parity on Android. Devin monitored
    Slack for user feedback, ran pixel-for-pixel screenshot comparisons against
    the iOS app, and posted video/image proof back in Slack.
  - The 44-Day Output:
      - 344,651 lines of Kotlin written.
      - 1,134 PRs merged (999 merged with zero human approval).
      - 2,841 screenshot tests & 1,217 tickets closed.
      - 98.5% of all commits written by Devin.
      - Shipped as a real product in open testing on Google Play (not throwaway
        prototype code).
  - What it took from the human (Samyok):
      - Only 183 messages (1,961 words total)—roughly 6 PRs merged per 1 human
        message.
      - On 22 of the 44 days (half the entire project!), Samyok sent zero
        messages—yet Devin still merged 24 to 57 PRs per day autonomously.
      - Many of his messages weren't technical instructions at all, just
        high-level steering like "be more ambitious."

How Both Talks Fit Together (The Big Picture)

If you combine Lauren Tan's talk and Walden Yan's talk, a unified playbook for
AI-native engineering emerges:

| Dimension           | Lauren Tan (Grok Bot / SpaceX–xAI)                                                   | Walden Yan (Devin / Cognition)                                                            |
| :------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Core Philosophy** | Engineer **trust** via deterministic codebases & heavy verification.                 | Build a **self-driving Software Factory** & delegate **outcomes/jobs**, not tasks.        |
| **Codebase Design** | Ban nuance (`useEffect`), ban comments, enforce strict directory imports (`dune`).   | Localize everything (`docker compose` + mocked 3rd parties) into **hourly VM snapshots**. |
| **Verification**    | 70% of tokens on verification: custom CLIs, CDP, TLA+, Lean, Bombadil fuzzing.       | Self-maintaining `SKILL.md` tests, pixel-for-pixel UI diffs, video proof in Slack.        |
| **Scaling & Loop**  | Fix the inner loop first, then attach outer triggers (`sandcastle` release fuzzing). | Vertical 2–3 person pods + **analyzer agents** recursively optimizing CI/costs/skills.    |
| **Outcome**         | \~11,000 PRs/month, 0.47% revert rate, faster Electron app perf.                     | 40.4x PR volume with 2.7x headcount, 2.8x GPU inference TPM, 344k-line Android app.       |

Bottom line from Walden: Once your sandbox, verification, and feedback loops are
in place, your team isn't bottlenecked by coding speed anymore—you're only
limited by how ambitious your goals are.
